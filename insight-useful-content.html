Strategy

Write for the question behind the search

Understanding intent helps turn a page of keywords into a useful customer experience.

RRizqOne EditorialOctober 1, 20265 min read
A BETTER QUESTION“What are
they really
trying to do?”START WITH INTENT

A keyword can tell you what somebody typed. It does not always tell you what they need. The useful question for a business is: what is the person trying to understand, compare or accomplish when they search?

One phrase can mean different things

Imagine someone searches for “website audit”. They may be looking for a definition, a do-it-yourself checklist, a technical tool or a provider. If your page jumps straight to a sales pitch, it may miss the reader’s immediate need.

Looking at the words around a query, the pages you already have and the questions customers ask in real conversations can help you form a better picture of intent. Search result pages can provide clues, too, though they are not a perfect map of every visitor.

Build the page around a useful task

Before drafting, write a simple sentence describing what the visitor should be able to understand or do after reading. Then plan the information in a natural order: give the short answer or context, explain the details, clarify limitations and offer a relevant next step.

  • Explain the topic directly. Don’t make visitors search the page for the point.
  • Use meaningful headings. Organise information according to the reader’s questions, not just your list of keywords.
  • Show what makes your advice specific. Add relevant examples, expertise and context you can stand behind.
  • Make the next step appropriate. A guide can link to a related service without turning every paragraph into a sales pitch.

Make it easy to navigate and trust

Useful writing is only part of the experience. Clear page titles, descriptive links, readable mobile layouts and accessible controls all help people find their way. Keep names, service descriptions and key business facts consistent across your website.

Review old content when services or policies change. Remove claims that are no longer accurate and add details when the questions you receive reveal a gap. A content plan should be a living tool for answering real needs, not an obligation to publish something every week.

Look beyond the page view

A visit can show that a page was seen; it does not automatically show whether it helped. Where possible, combine analytics with customer feedback, enquiry quality and the questions your team continues to hear. Use that information to improve the page and its next step.

Good content respects the reader’s time. Start with a genuine question, answer it as clearly as you can, and make it simple for someone to decide where to go from there.
